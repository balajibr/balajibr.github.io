Career notes

Twenty-seven years of work, 1999 to now, and the detours that stuck.

Working draft

Wipro 99–01 Honeywell 01–05 Microsoft 05–18 Google 18–23 Onix 23– What carried over

Twenty-seven years is long enough that the industry I started in and the one I work in today share very little vocabulary.

Writing this down, I noticed two threads I had not consciously connected before. One is making complicated information findable. The other is automating the judgment that sits between two steps that are already automated. Almost everything below is one or the other.

The boxed sections are asides. Blue ones are technical, the tangents and the things I only understood the value of later. Warm ones are personal and have nothing to do with the work at all.

Wipro · 1999 to 2001Structured documents

  • Worked with
  • DynaWeb (Enigma)
  • SGML
  • XML
  • DTDs
  • dynamic stylesheets
  • SVG
  • IETM
  • ATA aviation data specs
  • full-text search indexing
  • Netscape / IE 4-5

I started on DynaWeb, Enigma's distributed content management and e-publishing system. We were tailoring it for Bombardier Aerospace, and the job was getting them off paper. What came out the other side were Interactive Electronic Technical Manuals for the CRJ and Q400 fleets: maintenance manuals, wiring manuals, illustrated parts catalogs.

Underneath it was structured SGML, held to aviation industry data standards. It served over corporate intranets and extranets, and also shipped on disc, so a mechanic in a hangar with no network could still pull up the manual.

The engineering problem was formatting on the fly. Raw structured data went in, and stylesheets applied presentation dynamically depending on which browser was asking. In 1999 that meant fighting Netscape and Internet Explorer at the same time. Long compilation times, unforgiving DTDs, and a document model that had no tolerance for sloppiness, because the documentation described aircraft.

The piece I remember most clearly is the illustrations. We embedded vector graphics, catalogued them, and pushed them into the search index. Someone working on a complex engine assembly could search for a single screw or bolt and zoom straight to it. That was really the point of the whole project. We were building a structure precise enough that any individual component on an aircraft became addressable.

I did not think of it that way at the time. It was a retrieval problem over structured technical data. I would end up back at that same problem twice more.

Tech detour

The gems I did not know I was holding

DynaWeb had a lineage. Its predecessor, DynaText, came out of academic research, went to Inso, and then to Enigma, which is where I picked it up.

Somewhere in that inheritance I found out that Charles Goldfarb, who invented SGML, had a hand in one of the parsers we were working on. That part I can still check. My copy of The XML Handbook is on the shelf, and Goldfarb writes about DynaText in it. One of the forefathers of XML was in there somewhere too, though after this long I could not tell you whether I read it in a code comment or just picked it up in conversation.

The code was COM, written in C rather than C++. Doing COM that way means wiring vtables by hand and staying very careful about reference counts. Parts had been moved to C++ later for structure, so a single afternoon could put you in two eras of the same codebase.

Years later at Microsoft, when I learned COM properly, I kept recognising things. Same shapes, same discipline, picked up early without knowing what it was.

At the time I had no idea any of it was a gem.

Detour

My first flight was an interrogation

I had never been on a plane before Wipro sent me to Israel. First flight of my life, Mumbai to Tel Aviv, headed for Enigma's office in Herzliya Pituach.

Nobody warned me about El Al.

Security started with me. Where was I going, what would I be doing there, who did I work for. Then my family: names, what they did, where they lived, going back a lot further than I expected anyone to care about. Then it moved to the people I was travelling with, and that is the part I still think about. They asked what I knew about my colleagues. Their backgrounds, where they were from, whatever I happened to know.

Then they asked my colleagues the same questions about me, and about each other, and checked whether all our answers lined up.

I have flown a lot since. Nothing has come close.

Honeywell · 2001 to 2005What production actually means

  • Worked with
  • Production Planning
  • Production Analyst
  • Operations Management
  • dynamic OLAP cubes
  • refinery planning models
  • remote automation

Refinery and oil enterprise software. Production planning, production analysis, operations management. Dynamic cubes for production modeling, workflow automation for refinery planning, and remote automation products for plants that could not simply be restarted.

That last part is what stuck. A bug in that world could take a process unit offline and cost a plant hours of production. You learn to think about failure modes before features, and "it works on my machine" stops meaning anything.

Everything I later took for granted about production discipline, I learned here first, in a place where the physical world was downstream of the code.

Tech detour

Ajax, before anyone called it that

Microsoft's Outlook Web Access team built XMLHTTP so a browser could fetch from the server without repainting the page. It shipped inside Internet Explorer 5 in 1999. That was the first time a web page could behave the way a desktop application did.

I borrowed the technique for one of our monitoring dashboards. The praise I got for how fluid it felt ran well past the amount of code it actually took.

Then Gmail shipped and went all in on the same idea, someone finally gave the pattern a name in 2005, jQuery sanded off the rough edges, and it hardened into a standard everyone now assumes was always there.

For a few years in the middle it simply looked like magic.

Thirteen years, and they line up almost exactly with the industry's move to cloud. For the back half of it the strategy had a name inside the company, mobile first and cloud first, and both halves of that showed up in the work.

Software engineering lead for Managed Services for Cloud, running multi-tenant solutions across Azure IaaS, PaaS, and O365. Hybrid storage resource providers for Azure Stack, back when it was still called Windows Azure Pack. Cloud hydration with System Center DCS, doing data-driven multi-tier workload deployment through Azure Resource Manager and SCVMM.

The change here was altitude. At Honeywell I built applications. At Microsoft I built the thing other people built applications on. Those are different jobs. In platform work your users are engineers, your failure modes are other teams' outages, and your best decisions are the ones nobody ever notices. You also spend months arguing about abstractions, because the abstraction is the product.

Detour

A new phone every year, and some that did not exist yet

Mobile first meant the company was all in on devices, and the dogfood culture came with hardware. A new phone landed in my hands most years. Some of them were unreleased, handed out so we would carry them daily and file whatever broke. Using a device nobody outside the building had seen yet does not get old.

For about three years there was a gadget on top of the phone. A tablet one year, a laptop another, an Xbox 360 another.

Those were fun years.

Detour

TechReady, and taking the long way to Redmond

I spoke at TechReady, the annual internal sales event, which put me on a flight to Redmond every year through my prime years there.

The early trips were the good ones, mostly because the routing was terrible in the best way. Once through Malaysia, many times through Singapore, always with a layover long enough to leave the airport and actually go see the place. I collected a lot of cities in transit.

The offsites went to Sri Lanka and Thailand.

And once to Greece. That one was a thrill.

Google · 2018 to 2023Consulting, and documents again

  • Worked with
  • Document AI
  • Google Cloud Platform
  • document intelligence pipelines
  • Meena (internal dogfood)
  • RFP automation
  • pipeline analytics
  • partner enablement

Five years as a solutions architect. Customized Document AI processors for lending document types, and acting as a feedback loop back into product engineering on the document intelligence roadmap. Automated RFP response systems and pipeline analytics. Technical strategy and enablement for GSI partners across Data Analytics and AI/ML.

Two things were new. The first was that the work faced outward, through more layers than I expected. I was rarely in front of the end customer. The chain ran from Google to a global systems integrator to their client, and the GSI called us on the problems they could not solution themselves. I was the Google person expected to arrive with the answer.

There is rarely a single right solution, and sometimes ours was the wrong fit. Saying that out loud gets complicated when you are the one wearing the badge. My rule was to put the customer first and to be precise about what our stack does and, more usefully, what it does not do. Get that second part right and the partner reaches the correct conclusion on their own, without a Google engineer ever having to tell them to go buy something else. There were plenty of calls like that. I underestimated how different all of it is from engineering until I had to do it.

The second was the return to documents, and that one I went looking for. The Document AI work was a 20% project, the slice of the week Google let you spend on something you picked yourself. Twenty years after the aircraft manuals I was back to pulling structure out of documents so a machine could act on it.

There was still a schema. Lending paperwork is form-driven and well specified. What had moved was the point where the structure got enforced. In 1999 it was enforced at authoring time, and you wrote to the DTD or the parser threw you out. In lending, the schema describes where the values are supposed to land, and then a human with a pen decides how close to that they feel like getting.

So the work was recovery rather than parsing. Which box does this value actually belong to. Is that digit a seven or a one. Someone strikes out a number and writes the corrected loan amount on the same line as the application docket number, and any human reading it knows immediately what happened. A model has to be taught that a strikethrough means retract, that being next to something on the page implies nothing, and that the value it is looking for is now sitting in a field reserved for something else entirely.

Most of the gap between a model that scores well on a benchmark and a processor that survives real documents lives right there.

Detour

I cleared the SWE bar, then learned I did not want the job

The 20% work had a door on it. Writing production code on Document AI meant going through Google's software engineering interviews first. They dropped the soft rounds and kept the technical grilling exactly as it stood for a full SWE hire. Same bar, same questions.

Clearing it is still one of the things I am happiest about from those years, and the engineering itself was genuinely good work.

It also told me something I had not fully admitted to myself. Full time, that job was wrong for me at that point. The on-call rotations, and returning to the same surface week after week, wore on me faster than I expected. Consulting kept putting something unfamiliar in front of me every few months, and it turned out I wanted that more than I wanted the code.

Useful thing to find out by passing a test rather than by failing one.

Tech detour

Meena, and YouTube without ads

Long before any of the public frenzy, Google had meena.google.com running internally. You talked to the model and rated what came back, which is the human feedback loop that later became the standard way these things get tuned. I dogfooded it constantly and found it fascinating.

There was also an incentive. Dogfooding Meena came with YouTube and no ads.

I would like to say we all signed up out of scientific curiosity. A good number of us signed up for the ads.

The exit was not my choice. In February 2023 Google restructured and cut staff globally, and I was in it.

The timing had a particular sting to it. ChatGPT was a few months old and already everywhere. Bard, which is now Gemini, was being stood up inside the building I was on my way out of. I had spent years around Google's models, including the conversational work above, and I left right as the thing I most wanted to work on started properly. Missing the show from inside the theatre is a strange feeling.

What I did not see coming is that leaving is what got me to the work. Onix is where I picked up the LLM engineering I had been circling, and none of the last three years happens if I stay.

Onix · 2023 to nowBuilding again, and the patents

  • Worked with
  • ADK
  • LangGraph
  • Apache Airflow-style DAGs
  • vision language models
  • fine-tuned SLMs
  • RAG / knowledge catalogs
  • HITL evals
  • Google Cloud Platform

AI/ML architect. Rapid prototypes carried through into production agentic workflows. Vision language models for legacy system interaction, which mostly means getting a model to operate software that has no API. Fine-tuned small language models for domain work. Agentic orchestration with ADK, LangGraph, and custom frameworks built on Airflow-inspired DAGs.

The retrieval thread shows up here as knowledge catalogs: getting enterprise data into a shape an autonomous system can reason over. Same problem as the parts catalog, three technology generations later. The thing being indexed changed. So did the consumer, from a mechanic to a model. The question stayed put: how do you make a large, messy, structured corpus addressable by something that has to act on it?

The three patents come out of this period and the cloud migration work around it. They share one observation. The individual capabilities all existed already, but nobody had automated the judgment in between them. Migration assessment tools could inventory an estate but could not decide the wave plan. Scanners could find vulnerabilities but could not predict what would break if you fixed them. Both times the missing piece was the reasoning step in the middle, and both times it turned out to be patentable because nobody had built it as one chain.

That habit, looking for the human judgment sitting between two automated steps, is the most portable thing I have picked up. It is also more or less what agentic systems are for.

The claim maps for all three are on this site if you want the detail.

What carried over

None of the Wipro stack survives in what I do now. Neither does the Honeywell one. What survives is narrower and more useful than any technology.

Production discipline from industrial software, where a bug had the physical world downstream of it. Abstraction design from platform work, where the abstraction is the product. Translation from consulting.

And underneath all of it, one long argument with the same problem. How do you make a complicated body of information addressable by something that needs to act on it, whether that something is a mechanic holding a wrench or an agent holding a tool call.
